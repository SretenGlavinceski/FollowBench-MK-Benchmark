### 1. The Core Argument: Shareholder Primacy
Friedman argued that a corporation is not a social institution; it is a private enterprise owned by stockholders. Therefore, the executive's sole responsibility is to act as a "trustee" for the owners. 

He posited that:
*   **Profit is the metric of success:** A business must operate within the "rules of the game" (obeying all laws and ethical norms), but it should not pursue social goals (like poverty alleviation or environmental protection) unless they directly contribute to profitability.
*   **The "Taxation" Argument:** Friedman argued that if a CEO spends company resources on social causes, they are essentially imposing a "hidden tax" on the shareholders without democratic consent.

### 2. Assessment of Impact on Societal Ethics
The impact of Friedman’s doctrine on societal ethics is deeply polarizing, creating a tension between **deontological ethics** (duty-based) and **utilitarianism**.

*   **The Pro-Friedman View (Economic Efficiency):** Proponents argue that by maximizing profit, businesses create jobs, provide goods and services, and drive innovation. *   **The Critique (Externalities):** Critics argue that Friedman’s view ignores "externalities"—costs imposed on society that are not reflected in the price of a product (e.g., pollution, worker exploitation, or systemic inequality). By prioritizing profit above all, a business may ethically justify actions that degrade the social fabric, provided those actions remain "legal."

---

### 3. Consequence on Community Relations: The "License to Operate"
One significant consequence of prioritizing profit over community welfare is the **erosion of the "Social License to Operate."** When a business views the community solely as a source of labor or a market for consumption—rather than a stakeholder—it risks a breakdown in trust. This can lead to:
*   Hostility toward corporate expansion.
*   Increased regulatory scrutiny (as a reaction to perceived corporate greed).
*   A "me-first" corporate culture that prioritizes short-term gains over long-term sustainability.

A pertinent case study illustrating the consequences of prioritizing profit over societal ethics is the **Purdue Pharma** case (the makers of OxyContin).

*   **Implications:** This case serves as a critique of Friedman’s doctrine. It demonstrates that when a business focuses *exclusively* on profit without an internal ethical framework for social responsibility, it can produce "socially destructive" outcomes.
