Friedman's perspective fundamentally shifts the definition of corporate ethics from **stakeholder theory** (considering employees, communities, and the environment) to **shareholder primacy**.

*   **Influence on Ethical Standards:** Under the Friedman doctrine, ethical behavior is redefined strictly as **legal compliance**. An action is considered "ethical" if it adheres to the law and standard commercial practices; any action beyond that (such as donating to charity or reducing carbon emissions beyond legal requirements) is viewed as an inefficient allocation of resources that dilutes returns for shareholders. This creates a utilitarian ethical framework where the "greatest good" is defined solely by the maximization of financial return for owners, potentially marginalizing the moral obligations owed to society at large.
*   **Repercussion on Community Relationships:** A primary repercussion of this philosophy is the **erosion of trust and social cohesion** within communities. When businesses prioritize short-term profit maximization over community well-being, they may engage in practices that externalize costs onto the public, such as polluting local waterways, suppressing wages, or avoiding taxes. This leads to a transactional relationship between business and community, where the community is viewed merely as a resource to be exploited rather than a partner to be nurtured. This dynamic often results in social fragmentation, as communities feel abandoned by the very entities that hold significant economic power within them.

### Case Study: The 2010 Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill

A stark illustration of the tensions inherent in the Friedman doctrine is the **Deepwater Horizon oil spill** in 2010.

*   **Context:** The explosion of the Deepwater Horizon rig in the Gulf of Mexico was caused by cost-cutting measures and safety compromises made by BP (British Petroleum) to maximize short-term profits and meet quarterly targets.
*   **Application of Friedman's View:** Prior to the disaster, BP executives and analysts often cited the need to maintain low costs and high efficiency to satisfy shareholders, aligning with the idea that the company's only duty was to its owners. The decision to use cheaper cement and skip safety checks was a direct attempt to increase immediate profits.
*   **Implications:** The disaster resulted in the death of 11 workers, the destruction of the rig, and an unprecedented environmental catastrophe that devastated marine life and local fishing economies. While BP eventually paid billions in fines and cleanup costs, the initial prioritization of shareholder profit over safety and community welfare led to a catastrophic failure. The event highlighted the danger of Friedman's narrow definition of ethics: by treating safety and environmental stewardship as optional expenses rather than core ethical imperatives, the company caused immense harm to the community and the environment, ultimately damaging its own long-term viability and reputation.

### Supporting Expert Opinion

The limitations of the Friedman doctrine in the modern era are supported by the work of **R. Edward Freeman**, the founder of **Stakeholder Theory**.

Freeman argues that Friedman's view is too narrow and ignores the complex web of relationships a corporation is embedded in. In his influential book *A Stakeholder Approach to Corporate Social Responsibility* (1984), Freeman states:

> "The stakeholder approach to corporate social responsibility is based on the premise that the corporation is a nexus of interdependent relationships... The corporation has a responsibility to all of its stakeholders, not just its shareholders."
