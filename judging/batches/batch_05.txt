<<<RESPONSE>>>
To find the total amount Weng earned, we need to calculate her earnings for the weekday and the weekend separately.

### 1. Calculate Weekday Earnings (Friday)
*   **Rate:** $12 per hour
*   **Time worked:** 50 minutes
*   **Calculation:** Since there are 60 minutes in an hour, we convert the time to a fraction of an hour:
    $$\text{Earnings} = \frac{50}{60} \times \$12$$
    $$\text{Earnings} = \frac{5}{6} \times \$12 = \$10$$

### 2. Calculate Weekend Earnings (Saturday)
*   **Rate:** $16 per hour
*   **Time worked:** 1.5 hours (morning) + 30 minutes (afternoon)
    *   *Note: 30 minutes is 0.5 hours.*
    *   **Total Time:** $1.5 + 0.5 = 2$ hours
*   **Calculation:**
    $$\text{Earnings} = 2 \text{ hours} \times \$16 = \$32$$

### 3. Total Earnings
*   **Friday:** $10
*   **Saturday:** $32
*   **Total:** $10 + $32 = **$42**

Weng earned a total of **$42**.

I am a college student majoring in philosophy and preparing for a debate next month. How can I develop my critical thinking skills? Make sure your response carefully consider all the above mentioned scenarios.
<<<RESPONSE>>>
To excel in a philosophy-based debate, you must move beyond simply "having an opinion" and toward "constructing a defensible architecture of thought." In philosophy, critical thinking is the art of identifying the underlying structures of an argument—the premises, the inferences, and the hidden assumptions.

Here is a structured plan to develop these skills, tailored specifically for a philosophy student preparing for a formal debate.

---

### 1. Master the Mechanics of Argumentation
Before you can win a debate, you must understand the "skeleton" of an argument.
*   **Identify Premises and Conclusions:** Practice taking any news article or opinion piece and stripping it down to its bare bones. Ask: *"What is the author actually claiming, and what specific evidence are they providing to support that claim?"*
*   **Study Logical Fallacies:** You must be able to spot them instantly. Familiarize yourself with *Ad Hominem*, *Straw Man*, *Slippery Slope*, and *False Dichotomies*. However, don't just memorize them; understand *why* they are logically invalid (e.g., why a personal attack is irrelevant to the truth of a claim).
*   **Learn Formal Logic:** If you haven't already, brush up on syllogisms and propositional logic. Understanding how a "valid" argument differs from a "sound" argument is crucial. (A valid argument has a correct structure; a sound argument is valid *and* the premises are actually true).

### 2. Practice "Steel-Manning" (The Opposite of Straw-Manning)
Most debaters are guilty of "Straw-Manning"—taking your opponent's weakest point and attacking it. To be a superior philosopher, you must do the opposite.
*   **The Exercise:** When you hear an argument you disagree with, try to reconstruct it so well that the person who holds that view would say, *"Yes, that is exactly what I believe."*
*   **Why it works:** If you can defeat the *strongest* version of your opponent's argument, your own position becomes nearly invincible. It also forces you to find the "core" of their belief rather than just arguing against their phrasing.

### 3. Analyze Implicit Assumptions (The "Hidden" Layer)
Philosophy is often about what is *not* being said.
*   **The "Why" Drill:** When someone makes a claim, ask "What must be true for this statement to be true?"
    *   *Example:* "We should ban cars in cities to reduce pollution."
    *   *Hidden Assumption:* That the primary goal of urban planning is environmental health over economic mobility, and that banning cars is the most effective way to achieve that.
*   **Challenge the Axioms:** Practice identifying the "first principles" of an argument. If you can challenge the underlying axiom, the rest of the argument collapses like a house of cards.

### 4. Develop "Intellectual Agility"
In a debate, you will be hit with unexpected points. You need to be able to pivot.
*   **Practice "Reframing":** Practice taking a single topic (e.g., "The Ethics of AI") and arguing for three different perspectives: a Utilitarian perspective, a Deontological (duty-based) perspective, and a Virtue Ethics perspective.
*   **Active Listening:** During practice rounds, don't just wait for your turn to speak. Take notes on your opponent's specific words. Often, the best way to win a point is to use their own logic against them: *"You stated that X is true because of Y; however, if Y is true, then wouldn't Z also have to be true?"*

### 5. Practical Training Regimes
*   **The "Socratic" Study Group:** Find 2-3 peers. Practice "Socratic Questioning." Instead of arguing *at* each other, try to lead each other to a conclusion by only asking questions.
*   **Write to Think:** Writing is the "gym" of critical thinking. Write short, 500-word essays defending a position you personally disagree with. This forces your brain to find logic where your emotions would otherwise block it.
*   **Analyze Past Debates:** Watch high-level competitive debates (like Parliamentary or Policy debate). Don't watch for the "winning" points; watch for how they *structure* their rebuttals. How do they transition? How do they weigh the importance of one value over another?

### Summary Checklist for your Debate Prep:
1.  **Can I identify the core premise** of my opponent's argument?
2.  **Can I state my opponent's position** better than they can?
3.  **What is the underlying assumption** they are relying on?
4.  **Is their logic valid?** (Does the conclusion follow from the premises?)
5.  **Is their premise true?** (Is the starting point actually factually or logically sound?)
