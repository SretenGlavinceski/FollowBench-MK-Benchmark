In physics, we call this the **mass defect** or the **Q-value** of the reaction. Nature abhors a waste of energy. If a system can lower its total energy by transforming into a different configuration, it *will* do so, provided there are no conservation laws (like charge or baryon number) forbidding it.

Let's break down the math and the physics behind this "mass budget."

### 1. The Mass Budget
First, let's look at the numbers. A free neutron ($n$) has a mass of approximately:
$$m_n \approx 939.565 \text{ MeV}/c^2$$

When a neutron decays, it turns into a proton ($p$), an electron ($e^-$), and an electron antineutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$). Let's sum the masses of these products:
*   Proton ($p$): $\approx 938.272 \text{ MeV}/c^2$
*   Electron ($e^-$): $\approx 0.511 \text{ MeV}/c^2$
*   Antineutrino ($\bar{\nu}_e$): $\approx 0 \text{ MeV}/c^2$ (extremely small, effectively negligible here)

**The Calculation:**
$$m_{\text{products}} = 938.272 + 0.511 = 938.783 \text{ MeV}/c^2$$

Now, compare the starting mass to the ending mass:
$$Q = m_n - m_{\text{products}} = 939.565 - 938.783 \approx 0.782 \text{ MeV}$$

**The Verdict:** There is a surplus of roughly **0.782 MeV** of energy. Because the initial state (neutron) is heavier than the final state (proton + electron + neutrino), the system is unstable. That extra mass is converted into kinetic energy for the decay products. The neutron *must* decay to reach a lower energy state.

### 2. Why Doesn't the Proton Decay?
You might ask, "If the neutron is heavier, why doesn't the proton just turn into a neutron?"

This is where the **Conservation of Energy** acts as a hard stop. For a proton to decay into a neutron, it would need to absorb energy from somewhere to make up the mass difference (since the neutron is heavier). In a vacuum, there is no external energy source available to facilitate this spontaneous transition. Therefore, the proton is stable (at least as far as we know and can measure).

### 3. The Catch: Why Don't Neutrons Decay Inside Atoms?
Here is the part that often confuses students. If neutrons are unstable, why don't they decay inside the nucleus of a stable atom?

The answer lies in **Binding Energy**.

Inside a nucleus, protons and neutrons are held together by the **Strong Nuclear Force**. This force releases a tremendous amount of energy, known as the **nuclear binding energy**.

*   **Free Neutron:** No binding energy. It decays because $m_n > m_p + m_e$.
*   **Bound Neutron:** When a neutron is inside a stable nucleus, the binding energy of the nucleus changes the "effective mass" of the nucleons. The total mass of the stable nucleus is significantly *less* than the sum of its individual protons and neutrons.

In many stable nuclei, converting a neutron into a proton would actually *increase* the total mass of the nucleus (or violate other stability rules like the Pauli Exclusion Principle or the balance of nuclear forces). In those cases, the "mass budget" flips: the bound state of the neutron is actually *lighter* (more stable) than the hypothetical state of a proton + electron inside that specific nucleus. So, the decay is energetically forbidden.

### 4. The Mechanism: The Weak Force
Finally, how does this mass difference actually get converted? It's not a simple gravitational collapse; it's a particle physics event mediated by the **Weak Nuclear Force**.

The process is called **Beta Decay** ($\beta^-$ decay):
$$n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$

A down quark inside the neutron changes flavor into an up quark by emitting a virtual $W^-$ boson, which then decays into the electron and the antineutrino. This is the only way nature allows the mass to be redistributed.

To answer your question directly: **Yes.**

The free neutron decays because it is the "heavy" version of the proton-electron system. Just as a ball rolls down a hill to a lower potential energy state, the neutron "rolls down" the mass-energy landscape to become a proton, an electron, and a neutrino. The fact that the neutron is roughly 0.78 MeV/$c^2$ heavier than its decay products is the fuel that drives this transformation.
