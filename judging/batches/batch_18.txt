In the view of Milton Friedman, the primary responsibility of a business is to **maximize profits for its shareholders.**

This perspective, famously articulated in his 1970 essay, *"The Social Responsibility of Business is to Increase its Profits,"* argues that a corporation is not a social institution, but a private enterprise. Therefore, its sole purpose is to provide goods and services in a way that generates the highest possible return for the owners (shareholders) while staying within the bounds of the law and the "rules of the game."

### Evaluation of Influence on Societal Ethics
Friedman’s philosophy has had a profound and polarizing impact on societal ethics, primarily centered on the concepts of **fiduciary duty** and **property rights**:

1.  **The "Agent" Argument:** Friedman argues that corporate executives are agents of the shareholders. If a CEO spends company resources on social causes (e.g., environmental cleanup or social justice programs), they are essentially spending the shareholders' money without their consent. Friedman views this as a breach of trust—equivalent to a person hiring a real estate agent to sell a house and then asking the agent to donate the commission to charity.
2.  **The "Taxation" Argument:** Friedman posits that if a business wants to solve social problems, it should do so through government policy (taxation) or private philanthropy. By allowing corporations to self-regulate their social goals, they are essentially creating a "hidden tax" on consumers and employees, bypassing the democratic process.
3.  **Ethical Critique:** Critics argue that this "shareholder primacy" ignores the **stakeholder theory**, which suggests that businesses have moral obligations to employees, customers, and the environment. Critics argue that by focusing solely on profit, businesses may externalize costs (like pollution) onto society, leading to a "race to the bottom" in ethical standards.

### Repercussion on Community Relations: The "Externalities" Conflict
One significant repercussion of Friedman’s philosophy on community relations is the **prioritization of profit over local well-being**, often manifesting as the neglect of "externalities." When a business views its only duty as profit, it may view community needs as obstacles to be bypassed rather than partners to be supported. This can lead to a breakdown in trust, where the community views the corporation as a "predatory" entity rather than a "neighbor."

#### Case Study: Theized (or "The Sackler" approach) vs. The "Good Neighbor" Model
A relevant context can be found in the **Purdue Pharma** case (the makers of OxyContin). While not a direct application of Friedman’s theory in a vacuum, it represents the logical extreme of prioritizing profit over social responsibility.

*   **Context:** Purdue Pharma focused intensely on maximizing profits through aggressive marketing of opioids. By adhering to a narrow interpretation of "business success" (profit maximization) and ignoring the societal "cost" of addiction, the company achieved massive wealth for shareholders.
*   **Community Repercussion:** The result was a catastrophic breakdown in community relations. The company faced massive litigation, lost its social license to operate, and caused a public health crisis that devastated communities.
*   **Analysis:** This case illustrates the tension in Friedman’s theory: while a business *is* legally obligated to seek profit, when that profit is derived from ignoring the ethical health of the community, it leads to systemic collapse. It highlights the modern shift toward **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)**, where companies realize that long-term profitability is often impossible without maintaining a positive, ethical relationship with the community.
