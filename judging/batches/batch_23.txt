The short answer is **yes**, these four ions ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{OH}^-$, and $\text{HCO}_3^-$) can coexist in a water solution.

However, their coexistence is governed by the principles of chemical equilibrium, specifically regarding **pH** and **solubility**.

### 1. Chemical Behavior and Interactions
When these ions are dissolved in water, they do not typically react to form a solid precipitate or a gas (under standard conditions). Instead, they exist in a state of dynamic equilibrium.

*   **$\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$:** These are spectator ions. They do not react with water or each other. They simply provide the ionic strength of the solution.
*   **$\text{OH}^-$ and $\text{HCO}_3^-$:** These are the "active" components of the mixture. They interact through the **Carbonate Buffer System** equilibrium:
    $$\text{HCO}_3^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{CO}_3^{2-}$$
    $$\text{OH}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
    Because $\text{OH}^-$ is a strong base and $\text{HCO}_3^-$ is a weak base (amphiprotic), they will coexist in a stable equilibrium. The $\text{OH}^-$ ions will slightly shift the equilibrium, but they will not "neutralize" each other into water because the $\text{HCO}_3^-$ is not a strong acid.

### 2. The Role of pH
The coexistence of these ions is highly dependent on the pH of the solution:
*   **At Neutral to Basic pH (approx. 7.0 to 10.0):** This is the most stable range for this mixture. The $\text{HCO}_3^-$ remains largely intact, and the $\text{OH}^-$ concentration is relatively low. This is similar to the chemistry of seawater or biological fluids.
*   **At Very High pH (Highly Alkaline):** If you add an excess of $\text{OH}^-$, the $\text{HCO}_3^-$ will convert into carbonate ($\text{CO}_3^{2-}$) and eventually into carbonate ions.
*   **At Very Low pH (Acidic):** If the solution becomes acidic, the $\text{HCO}_3^-$ will react with $\text{H}^+$ ions to form $\text{CO}_2$ gas and water, causing the bicarbonate to disappear from the solution.

### 3. Solubility Considerations

*   **Sodium Bicarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$):** Highly soluble in water.
*   **Sodium Carbonate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$):** Highly soluble in water.

Because both salts are highly soluble, the ions will remain dissolved in the water rather than forming a solid "crust" or precipitate, provided the concentration is not extremely high (supersaturated).

What, in the view of Milton Friedman, constitutes the primary responsibility of a business? Additionally, evaluate its influence on societal ethics and highlight one possible repercussion on community relations, incorporating one relevant case study for context.
<<<RESPONSE>>>
In the view of Milton Friedman, the primary responsibility of a business is singular and unequivocal: **to maximize profits for its shareholders**, provided it operates within the framework of the law and adheres to ethical customs.

Friedman articulated this most famously in his 1970 New York Times magazine essay, *"The Social Responsibility of Business is to Increase Its Profits."* He argued that in a free enterprise system, the only social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. He contended that when corporate executives use corporate resources to spend money on social causes (such as charity or political advocacy) without the consent of the shareholders, they are effectively engaging in an act of "theft." In his view, executives are employees of the shareholders, and spending other people's money on social ends is akin to a dictator spending the nation's money on social programs without the consent of the electorate. For Friedman, the role of the corporation is not to be a "citizen" with social obligations, but rather a mechanism for efficient resource allocation that serves the interests of its owners.

### Influence on Societal Ethics

Friedman's perspective fundamentally challenges the concept of **Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)** as a moral imperative for business leaders. His influence on societal ethics is profound because it reframes ethical behavior not as a duty of the corporation itself, but as a constraint imposed by the legal system and the moral compass of the individual executives.

*   **The "Two-Level" Ethics:** Friedman suggests a dichotomy where the *business* has no social responsibility, but the *individuals* running the business do. He argues that if a business wants to engage in social good, it must do so through the proper channels: the shareholders (via dividends) or the employees (via wages), or by the executives using their own personal funds.
*   **Market Efficiency vs. Paternalism:** This view posits that the market is the most efficient mechanism for addressing social needs. If a company decides to solve a social problem (e.g., pollution or poverty) rather than maximize profit, it is arguably distorting the market and making decisions that the shareholders did not authorize. Consequently, Friedman's philosophy tends to prioritize **utilitarian efficiency** over **distributive justice** or **stakeholder theory**, suggesting that the pursuit of profit, when regulated by law, inherently leads to the best societal outcomes.

### Repercussion on Community Relations

A significant repercussion of adhering strictly to Friedman's doctrine is the potential erosion of **trust and social license to operate** within a community. When businesses prioritize short-term profit maximization over community welfare, they risk being perceived as exploitative, leading to strained relationships, regulatory backlash, and reputational damage. Communities may feel abandoned, leading to protests, boycotts, or aggressive lobbying for stricter government regulations that could stifle the very business growth Friedman advocated.

#### Case Study: The Flint Water Crisis and Corporate Accountability

While the Flint Water Crisis is primarily a government failure, it serves as a potent case study for the *repercussions* of a business environment that prioritizes cost-cutting (profit maximization) over community welfare, a dynamic often criticized by Friedman's detractors and supported by his critics.

In 2014, the City of Flint, Michigan, switched its water source to the Flint River to save money (a direct application of cost-cutting/profit maximization logic applied to public infrastructure). The city failed to treat the water with corrosion inhibitors, leading to lead leaching from aging pipes into the drinking water supply.

*   **The Business/Corporate Angle:** While the city is public, the decision-making process mirrored the logic Friedman criticized: prioritizing immediate fiscal savings (maximizing the "profit" of the taxpayer) over the long-term health and safety of the citizens.
*   **Repercussion on Community Relations:** The immediate repercussion was a catastrophic loss of trust. The community felt betrayed by the very entities (government and private water contractors) responsible for their safety. This led to:
    1.  **Social Unrest:** Protests and civil disobedience.
    2.  **Regulatory Backlash:** Federal intervention and lawsuits that cost billions, arguably more than the initial savings.
    3.  **Long-term Stigma:** The city and the state of Michigan faced enduring reputational damage, making future investment and community cooperation difficult.

**Conclusion:**
Had the decision-makers followed a stakeholder approach (the opposite of Friedman's strict shareholder primacy) and prioritized the community's health over immediate budgetary efficiency, the crisis might have been averted. The Flint case illustrates Friedman's warning in reverse: while he argued that businesses should not spend money on social issues, the *failure* to do so when social issues are critical can lead to disastrous consequences for community relations, ultimately harming the business environment itself through loss of trust and legal liability.
